# Neurodiversity Primer — What You Need to Know as Non-Medical Team Members
**Team Kaleidoscope | Reference for Partner Meetings and Solution Design**

---

## What "Neurodiversity" Actually Means

**Neurodiversity** is not a diagnosis — it is a framework. The concept, popularized by sociologist Judy Singer in 1998, holds that variation in how human brains work is natural and normal. Some brains process the world differently from the statistical average — those people are called **neurodivergent**. People whose brains align more closely with the average are called **neurotypical**.

**Important:** Neurodiversity is NOT a euphemism for "broken." It is a recognition that differences in thinking, learning, and communicating are part of human variation — not deficits to be cured. This framing is deeply embedded in AZACS and First Place AZ culture. If you use clinical language without care, partners will notice.

**Conditions considered neurodivergent** (not exhaustive):
- Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)
- Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)
- Dyslexia
- Dyscalculia
- Dyspraxia / Developmental Coordination Disorder (DCD)
- Tourette Syndrome
- Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD) — increasingly included
- Sensory Processing Disorder (SPD)
- Intellectual/Developmental Disabilities (I/DD) — partial overlap

For this hackathon, the primary conditions you will encounter are **autism**, **ADHD**, **intellectual/developmental disabilities**, and **executive function challenges** (which cut across all of the above).

## Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) — Deep Dive

### What It Is

Autism is a **neurodevelopmental condition** characterized by differences in social communication, sensory processing, and patterns of behavior. The word "spectrum" means it varies enormously in how it presents — two autistic people may look nothing alike.

**Diagnostic criteria (DSM-5):**
1. Persistent differences in social communication and interaction
2. Restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities
3. Present from early childhood (though may not be recognized until later)
4. Causes significant impact on daily functioning in at least some contexts

**Note on language:** The autistic community is split on "person-first" vs. "identity-first" language. Some prefer "person with autism" (person-first — separates identity from condition). Others prefer "autistic person" (identity-first — autism is part of who they are, not something tacked on). At AZACS and First Place, follow the lead of the individual. When in doubt, use "autistic" with adults and ask.

### The Spectrum — What "High" vs. "Low" Support Needs Actually Means

The old terms "high-functioning" and "low-functioning" are considered outdated and inaccurate — they describe how comfortable a situation is for the observer, not the person's actual experience or needs.

The current framework uses **support levels** defined by the DSM-5:

| Level | Also Called | What It Means |
|---|---|---|
| Level 1 | "Requires support" | Can manage most situations with some support; challenges more visible in social/communication contexts |
| Level 2 | "Requires substantial support" | More noticeable differences; requires more scaffolding; significant challenges in daily life |
| Level 3 | "Requires very substantial support" | Significant needs in communication, daily functioning, transitions; may be non-speaking |

AZACS serves all three levels across its three program tracks (Academic, Modified Academic, Functional). First Place AZ serves primarily **moderate support needs** (roughly Level 1–2).

### How Autism Shows Up in Day-to-Day Life

**Social communication differences:**
- May struggle with implicit social cues (facial expressions, tone of voice, sarcasm, unspoken rules)
- May take language very literally — "it's raining cats and dogs" could be genuinely confusing
- May have difficulty understanding others' mental states (theory of mind challenges)
- May communicate in atypical ways — echolalia (repeating phrases), scripts, or AAC devices

**Sensory processing differences:**
- Can be hypersensitive (over-reactive) or hyposensitive (under-reactive) to any sensory input
- Common triggers: fluorescent lights, background noise, unexpected touch, food textures, strong smells
- A student covering their ears or rocking is often self-regulating — it's not disruptive behavior, it's coping
- Sensory overload can look like a tantrum but is actually neurological overwhelm

**Executive function (see full section below):**
- Planning and initiating tasks is harder
- Transitions between activities are frequently difficult
- Unexpected changes cause genuine distress, not just preference

**Repetitive behaviors and special interests:**
- Stimming (self-stimulatory behavior): rocking, hand-flapping, spinning, humming — this is regulation, not a problem
- Intense interests in specific topics (sometimes called "special interests") — can be a bridge for engagement
- Rigid routines — deviation from expected routines causes real anxiety

**Communication range:**
- Approximately 30% of autistic people are minimally verbal or non-speaking — this does NOT mean they cannot understand or think
- Many use AAC (Augmentative and Alternative Communication): picture boards, speech-generating devices, apps like Proloquo2Go
- Non-verbal communication (typing, pointing, gesture) is full communication

## ADHD — What It Is and How It Shows Up

### What It Is

ADHD (Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder) is a neurodevelopmental condition affecting attention regulation, impulse control, and activity level. Despite the name, it is less about "not paying attention" and more about **inconsistent attention regulation** — people with ADHD can hyperfocus intensely on things they're interested in while struggling to focus on tasks that don't provide immediate stimulation.

**Three presentations:**
- **Predominantly Inattentive** (used to be called ADD): difficulty sustaining attention, forgetfulness, losing things, easily distracted, not following through — without hyperactivity
- **Predominantly Hyperactive-Impulsive:** fidgeting, inability to stay seated, talking excessively, difficulty waiting, acting before thinking
- **Combined:** both inattentive and hyperactive-impulsive features

**Co-occurrence with autism:** 30–80% of autistic people also meet criteria for ADHD. This combination is sometimes called "AuDHD." At AZACS, many students will have both.

### How ADHD Shows Up in Day-to-Day Life

- Difficulty starting tasks (task initiation paralysis) — can look like defiance but isn't
- Difficulty sustaining attention on non-preferred tasks
- Time blindness — genuinely cannot feel how long time is passing
- Working memory challenges — holding instructions in mind while acting on them
- Emotional dysregulation — emotions hit harder and faster; difficulty calming down
- Hyperfocus — can be deeply productive, but hard to interrupt
- Sleep issues common (circadian rhythm differences)

**At AZACS:** ADHD-related challenges overlap heavily with autism-related challenges. A teacher dealing with "a student who won't start their work" may be looking at executive function failure (autism) OR task initiation paralysis (ADHD) OR both.

## Executive Function — The Core Problem Domain

### What It Is

Executive function (EF) refers to a set of mental processes managed by the prefrontal cortex. Think of it as the brain's "management system" — it coordinates all the other systems.

**The 8 core executive functions (per neuropsychologist Dr. Peg Dawson):**

| Function | What It Governs |
|---|---|
| **Response inhibition** | Stopping yourself from acting before thinking |
| **Working memory** | Holding information in mind while using it |
| **Emotional control** | Managing feelings so they don't override behavior |
| **Sustained attention** | Maintaining focus on a task over time |
| **Task initiation** | Starting a task without procrastinating indefinitely |
| **Planning/prioritization** | Creating a roadmap; deciding what matters most |
| **Organization** | Keeping track of materials and information |
| **Time management** | Estimating, allocating, and tracking time accurately |
| **Goal-directed persistence** | Staying on course toward a goal despite distractions |
| **Flexibility** | Adapting when plans change; shifting between tasks |

**Why EF is central to this hackathon:** Both autism AND ADHD involve EF challenges. And EF is exactly what's required by the life demands we're asking neurodivergent people to meet — school transitions, job tasks, apartment management, medical appointments. There is a chronic mismatch between EF demands and EF capacity.

### How EF Challenges Show Up at AZACS (K–12)

- A student "won't start" their work → task initiation failure
- A student "loses it" when there's a schedule change → flexibility/emotional control failure
- A student can do a task with a teacher present but not independently → scaffolding dependency (working memory + organization)
- A student knows the rule but breaks it anyway → response inhibition difficulty
- A student can't remember multi-step instructions → working memory limit

### How EF Challenges Show Up at First Place AZ (Adults)

- Difficulty managing apartment routines (grocery shopping, laundry, bill paying)
- Getting to work on time, consistently
- Handling unexpected changes at work without escalating
- Navigating a doctor's appointment without a family member to speak for them
- Managing medication schedules
- Maintaining friendships (knowing when to reach out, how often, what to say)

## Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities (I/DD)

### What It Is

An **intellectual disability** (ID) is defined as significant limitations in both intellectual functioning (IQ below ~70) and adaptive behavior (practical, social, and conceptual skills) that originate before age 18. A **developmental disability** (DD) is broader — it includes ID but also encompasses cerebral palsy, Down syndrome, spina bifida, and other conditions originating in development.

**I/DD and autism overlap significantly** — approximately 30–40% of autistic individuals also have an intellectual disability. At AZACS, the Functional Academic program primarily serves students with co-occurring autism and I/DD.

**What this means for solution design:**
- Literacy may be limited or absent — interfaces must work without reading
- Abstract concepts (future dates, money, hypotheticals) are harder
- Repetition and routine are essential for learning
- Visual/pictorial systems work better than text-heavy ones
- Voice and AAC input/output are often more accessible than typing

## Dyslexia

### What It Is

Dyslexia is a **reading disorder** characterized by difficulty with accurate and fluent word recognition, poor spelling, and decoding difficulties — despite adequate intelligence and instruction. It stems from phonological processing differences (how the brain connects letters to sounds).

**How it shows up:**
- Difficulty reading aloud, reading slowly, avoiding reading
- Spelling errors that persist past when neurotypical peers have mastered spelling
- Difficulty with written output (ideas don't transfer to paper well)
- May appear "smart" orally but struggle on written tests
- Reading comprehension can be fine if text is read aloud

**At AZACS:** Dyslexia often co-occurs with ADHD and autism. Dylan Shloss's CKLA curriculum challenge is directly relevant — CKLA is a phonics-heavy literacy program, which is appropriate for many students but creates a specific mismatch for students with dyslexia who need different decoding approaches.

## Sensory Processing Disorder (SPD)

### What It Is

SPD is when the brain has difficulty organizing and responding to sensory input. It is not a standalone DSM diagnosis but is recognized as a significant feature of autism, ADHD, and several other conditions.

**Two main profiles:**
- **Sensory hypersensitivity (over-responsive):** Inputs that are background noise for most people feel overwhelming. A flickering light, the tag in a shirt, a crowded cafeteria — these can be genuinely painful or destabilizing.
- **Sensory hyposensitivity (under-responsive):** Needs MORE input to register sensations. May seek out intense movement, pressure, or sound. Stimming often provides this.

**Why it matters for solution design:**
- Screen brightness, notification sounds, haptic feedback, background colors — all of these can be sensory triggers or sensory needs
- A student who gets up and walks around the classroom is often seeking proprioceptive (body position) input, not being defiant
- AZACS has sensory rooms and sensory tools for this reason

## Communication Profiles — What You Might Encounter

| Profile | Description | What it means in a meeting |
|---|---|---|
| **Verbal, typical fluency** | Speaks in full sentences, initiates conversation | Most similar to neurotypical interaction; may still have pragmatic (social) communication differences |
| **Verbal, scripted** | Uses learned or memorized phrases; may repeat phrases heard before (echolalia) | Don't assume they understand what they're saying; responses may be scripted even if they seem appropriate |
| **Minimally verbal** | Speaks some words or short phrases but limited spontaneous communication | May understand more than they can express; use yes/no questions; look for communication device |
| **Non-speaking with AAC** | Uses a device, board, or app to communicate | Treat them as fully communicative; address them directly, not their support person; give processing time |
| **Non-speaking without AAC** | No consistent communication system established | Extremely difficult; look for behavioral signals; defer entirely to staff who know them |

**At First Place AZ:** Most residents are verbal. But verbal doesn't mean neurotypical communication — they may still have pragmatic language differences (indirect statements, difficulty with ambiguous language, challenges with back-and-forth conversation).

## Behavioral Terms You Will Hear — What They Actually Mean

| Term You'll Hear | What It Means |
|---|---|
| **Maladaptive behavior** | Any behavior that interferes with learning or safety — NOT the same as "bad behavior." It's a clinical descriptor, not a moral judgment |
| **Antecedent** | The trigger or event before a behavior (what sets it off) |
| **Behavior** | The observable action being measured |
| **Consequence** | What happens after the behavior — determines if it continues or stops |
| **ABC model** | Antecedent-Behavior-Consequence — the fundamental ABA analysis framework |
| **Reinforcement** | Anything that increases the likelihood a behavior will happen again |
| **Extinction** | Removing the reinforcement for a behavior to decrease it |
| **Prompt** | A cue or support that helps someone do something (verbal, gestural, physical) |
| **Prompt fading** | Gradually reducing prompts so the person becomes independent |
| **Meltdown** | Neurological overwhelm — not a tantrum. Person loses ability to regulate. Cannot be reasoned with in the moment. |
| **Shutdown** | The opposite of meltdown — withdrawal, going non-verbal, freezing. Also overwhelm, just directed inward. |
| **Stimming** | Self-stimulatory behavior — rocking, flapping, spinning, humming. Regulation mechanism, not a problem. |
| **Scripting** | Repeating memorized phrases from media/memory. Often used as communication or comfort. |
| **Eloping** | Running away from a setting — a significant safety concern in school settings |
| **De-escalation** | Reducing a person's emotional/behavioral arousal to prevent or end a meltdown |
| **Dysregulation** | State of emotional/behavioral overwhelm where self-regulation capacity is exceeded |

## What NOT to Say or Do — Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why It's a Problem | What to Say Instead |
|---|---|---|
| "She's high-functioning so she's almost normal" | Erases real challenges; "high-functioning" is not a diagnosis | "She has significant strengths in some areas and significant support needs in others" |
| "He doesn't look autistic" | Implies there's a "look" to autism; often dismisses real struggles | Nothing — this is never a helpful observation |
| Talking about the person to the caregiver instead of to them | Treats the person as an object, not a person | Address the autistic person directly, even if they need support to respond |
| "They're just doing it for attention" | Almost never true; behavior serves a function | "What need might this behavior be communicating?" |
| Calling First Place a "facility" | Deeply offensive to the culture and residents there | "community," "home," "residence," "building" |
| Treating stimming as a behavior to stop | Stimming is regulation — suppressing it causes distress | Design around it; don't eliminate it |
| Assuming non-verbal = doesn't understand | Many non-speaking people have full comprehension | Treat them as fully present; direct all communication to them |

## Co-Occurring Conditions — The Reality

Almost no one at AZACS or First Place has a single, clean diagnosis. The realistic picture:

- **Autism + ADHD** (AuDHD): Most common combination. EF challenges are compounded from both directions.
- **Autism + Anxiety**: Extremely common. Often the "behavior" that staff are managing is anxiety-driven, not attention-seeking.
- **Autism + ID**: ~30–40% of autistic individuals. Requires fundamentally different curriculum (Functional program at AZACS).
- **Autism + Sensory Processing**: Near-universal. Almost every autistic person has some sensory sensitivities.
- **Autism + Epilepsy**: ~20–30% of autistic people have epilepsy — higher than general population. Medication interactions and management are healthcare challenges.
- **Autism + Sleep disorders**: Very common; disrupted sleep worsens all other symptoms.

**Why this matters for your solution:** A tool that works for one person's autism may not work for another's because of different co-occurring conditions. **Design for range, not a single profile.**

## The Lifespan Problem — Why the Service Cliff Matters

This is the structural problem underpinning both AZACS and First Place AZ's work:

**Age 0–3:** Early intervention services (ABA, speech therapy, OT) — well-funded, well-understood  
**Age 3–22:** School services under IDEA (Individuals with Disabilities Education Act) — federally mandated  
**Age 22:** **The cliff.** Federal education mandates end. Adult services exist but are woefully underfunded, fragmented, and have waitlists measured in years (in Arizona, the DDD waitlist can be 3–10+ years).

**70,000–120,000** autistic individuals "age out" of school services every year in the US (Josh's figure). Most fall off this cliff into unemployment, isolation, and family caregiving dependency.

AZACS is preparing students for this transition. First Place AZ is catching some of the adults who fell. **The space between those two is where the biggest design opportunity lives.**

## Research and Literature You Should Know Exists

You don't need to read these — but if you cite relevant research in your pitch, judges will notice positively:

**Key frameworks:**
- **Social Model of Disability** — disability is caused by society's failure to accommodate, not the person's condition
- **Double Empathy Problem** (Dr. Damian Milton, 2012) — autistic people have difficulty reading neurotypical people, AND neurotypical people have difficulty reading autistic people. Communication is a two-way failure, not a one-way deficit.
- **Polyvagal Theory** (Dr. Stephen Porges) — autonomic nervous system states underlie fight/flight/freeze; relevant to understanding behavioral regulation

**Relevant statistics for your pitch:**
- 85% unemployment or underemployment rate for autistic adults (widely cited, varies by study)
- ~50% of autistic adults report co-occurring anxiety disorder
- Autistic adults visit the ER at much higher rates than neurotypical adults
- Average age of autism diagnosis in girls/women is significantly later than boys/men (masking)
- Life expectancy for autistic individuals is significantly lower than neurotypical average — mostly due to preventable causes (mental health, healthcare access)

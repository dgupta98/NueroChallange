## Who Is First Place AZ — The 60-Second Picture

First Place AZ is a residential community in central Phoenix where adults with autism and intellectual/developmental disabilities (A/I/DD) live independently with support. It is **not a facility, not a medical home, not assisted care** — residents sign a lease and choose to be there, just like any apartment. The building opened July 2018, is 18,000 sq ft, sits at Third Street and Catalina in central Phoenix (3 blocks from the light rail), and currently houses **up to 75 residents**.

They are also an **international education and training hub** through their Global Leadership Institute (GLI), which studies what works at First Place and builds models for other communities to replicate.

**Why they're your hackathon partner:** First Place represents the destination AZACS students are heading toward — adult life. The problems here are different: less about classroom structure and more about daily living, employment, healthcare navigation, and social isolation. Their speaker Josh explicitly asked teams to think beyond First Place residents to the **70,000–120,000 autistic individuals who transition to adulthood every year** and can't access a community like this.

## What First Place AZ Does — Full Detail

### The Three Components

**1. First Place Apartments**
- 55 studio, one-, two-, and four-bedroom units
- Up to 75 residents with A/I/DD (slides said 67 but Josh said "up to 75" including Transition Academy students)
- Residents sign a standard lease — they are tenants, not patients
- Supportive amenities are built into the building, not imposed on residents
- Residents are adults making their own choices

**2. First Place Transition Academy**
- A two-year, clinically guided program for individuals new to independent living
- Located on the first floor of the building
- Run in partnership with **SARRC** (Southwest Autism Research & Resource Center)

- Topics: employment, community engagement, self-determination, household and personal management
- Students also take courses at **Gateway Community College**
- Josh describes it as: **"Neurodivergent Adulting 101"**

**3. First Place Global Leadership Institute (GLI)**
- Josh's department
- Studies what works at First Place and builds replicable models for other cities
- Operates five research and training centers:

| Center | Focus |

## First Place Key Problem Areas (What to Design For)

### 1. Social Isolation and Loneliness
> "There is a level of isolation and loneliness that is faced in the neurodivergent population that is unprecedented."

What residents want most when they come to First Place:
1. **Friendships**
2. **Romantic relationships**
3. **Building community**
4. **Employment**

Most apps and tools ignore the social dimension. This is their #1 stated want.

---

### 2. Employment — Getting a Job vs. Keeping It

**Statistics:**
- 85% of First Place residents are either working or volunteering
- **Be Open, Be Ready** curriculum: 200 job seekers enrolled; **50% gainfully employed** (as of 2025)

**Josh's framing:**
> "It's one thing to get a job, it's even harder to keep it. We all know that, but certainly for the neurodivergent person it's even harder."

**Be Open, Be Ready curriculum goals:**
- Create incentive and skills in job seekers (not just ask employers to be inclusive)
- Prepare individuals to show up workforce-ready
- Support employment retention, not just placement

**Design opportunity:** What helps a neurodivergent adult navigate the social/executive function demands of keeping a job? (Handling feedback, managing schedule changes, communicating with coworkers, self-regulation under stress)

---

### 3. Healthcare Navigation

**The problem:**
- Healthcare environments are not designed for neurodivergent adults
- When they don't get proper primary care, they **flood the ER** — traumatic and expensive
- Physicians are not trained to work with neurodivergent adults (ongoing problem)
- Family members often speak for the adult in the doctor's office even when the adult could speak for themselves

**First Place's solution in progress:**
- **360 Health & Wellness Program** — 30 years in development; three-track curriculum:
  1. For autistic adults themselves
  2. For family members supporting them
  3. For healthcare professionals
- **Peace of Mind Portfolio** — accessible worksheets that help adults navigate physician appointments independently (visuals, lists, appointment prep templates)
- Medical elective partnership: Creighton University 4th-year medical students rotate through AZACS to learn autism-informed care

**Design opportunity:** Can AI help someone prepare for and navigate a medical appointment independently?

---

### 4. The Service Cliff

**What it is:** At age 22 (or 26 in some states), most federally funded special education and transition services end. Adults fall off a cliff of support with no equivalent system to catch them.

**Scale:** 70,000–120,000 autistic individuals transition to adulthood every year in the US. Most do NOT have access to anything like First Place.

**Josh's challenge to teams:**
> "I challenge you to think about the people that don't have the opportunity to live at First Place and what are ways that we can still build innovative solutions that support them regardless of where they're at and their level of supports."

**Design opportunity:** A solution built for First Place residents is nice. A solution that could work for any neurodivergent adult regardless of whether they have access to a supportive community is transformative.

---

### 5. Transportation and Community Access

- First Place is intentionally 3 blocks from Phoenix's light rail
- Partnership with **Waymo** for autonomous vehicle access to employment and community
- Transportation independence is a major barrier to employment for many residents

## Neurodiversity — What You Need to Know Before This Visit

The residents and participants at First Place AZ are **adults** with autism and intellectual/developmental disabilities. The challenges they face are different from children at AZACS — they are navigating employment, healthcare, and independent living, not school. Here is what to expect.

### Conditions You Will Encounter at First Place AZ

| Condition | What It Means for Adults Here |
|---|---|
| **Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)** | Social communication differences, sensory sensitivities, EF challenges, potential for literal language, need for routine and predictability. Residents are verbal adults but may still struggle with implicit social rules, reading social cues, or communication under stress. |
| **Intellectual Disability (ID, moderate)** | Some residents have co-occurring ID. Abstract concepts, future planning, and multi-step instructions may be harder. First Place serves **moderate** support needs — residents are not in 24/7 care. |
| **ADHD** | Time management, employment retention, organization. Getting to work on time, tracking responsibilities, handling workplace feedback. Josh specifically flagged keeping a job as harder than getting one — EF is why. |
| **Anxiety and Depression** | Very high rates in autistic adults — tied to social isolation, healthcare access, unemployment. A resident who seems reluctant or avoidant may be managing anxiety, not disinterest. |
| **Sensory Processing** | Still present in adults. Healthcare environments (bright lights, noise, touch) can be destabilizing. Some residents avoid doctors for this reason. |

### Executive Function in Adult Life — Why It's Harder Here

At AZACS, a teacher provides structure, prompts, and scaffolding all day. At First Place, adults are expected to manage their own lives. The EF demands of adult life are enormous:

| Adult Life Demand | EF Skill Required |
|---|---|
| Getting to work on time, every day | Time management, task initiation |
| Handling a schedule change at work | Flexibility, emotional control |
| Grocery shopping and meal prep | Planning, organization, working memory |
| Keeping a medical appointment | Task initiation, organization, sustained attention |
| Maintaining a friendship | Goal-directed persistence, social EF |
| Responding to feedback from a boss | Emotional regulation, response inhibition |
| Paying rent on time | Time management, planning, working memory |

This is the gap First Place is trying to bridge. Their residents are capable — they just need systems that reduce the EF load of daily life.

### Communication — Adults Are Different From Children

Most First Place residents are verbally fluent. But being verbal does not mean neurotypical communication:
- May struggle with ambiguous language, implied meaning, sarcasm
- May take statements literally — be direct and specific
- May need extra processing time to formulate responses — don't rush
- May be more comfortable with predictable conversation structure
- May use scripted or rehearsed responses in unfamiliar situations

**Critical cultural rule:** Speak directly to the resident, not about them to a support person. They are adults making their own choices. If a support staff member is present, they are there for assistance, not as spokesperson.

### The "Dignity of Risk" Principle — This Changes How You Design

First Place explicitly operates on the principle that adults with disabilities have the **right to take risks, make choices, and sometimes fail**. This is not carelessness — it is a philosophical commitment to autonomy.

What this means for your solution:
- Do NOT design paternalistic systems that prevent action or require caregiver approval
- Do NOT build anything that treats the user as a patient who needs to be protected from themselves
- DO design things that inform, support, and scaffold — then let the person decide
- DO include the user in controlling what is tracked, shared, and stored

### The Service Cliff — Scale Your Thinking Here

- **70,000–120,000** autistic individuals age out of school supports every year in the US
- Most have NO equivalent to First Place in their community
- Arizona DDD (adult services) waitlist: 3–10+ years
- First Place serves 75 people. The population needing support is millions.

Josh explicitly asked teams to design for people who don't have access to First Place. If your solution only works inside a supported residential community, it helps 75 people. If it works for any autistic adult navigating life independently, it helps millions.

### What NOT to Say or Do at First Place

| Don't | Why | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| Call it a "facility" | Deeply offensive — residents are tenants, not patients | "community," "building," "home," "residence" |
| Ask a support worker about a resident while the resident is present | Treats them as non-persons | Address the resident directly; the support worker is there for backup |
| Assume low verbal = low understanding | Many people have full comprehension with limited verbal output | Speak to them directly; slow down; yes/no questions if helpful |
| Design something requiring caregiver control | Undermines autonomy and dignity of risk | Put control with the resident |
| Call autism a "disease" | Autism is a neurological difference, not a disease | "Condition," "neurodivergent," or just "autism" |

### Key Terms for First Place Conversations

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **A/I/DD** | Autism and/or Intellectual/Developmental Disabilities |
| **Moderate support needs** | Can live independently with scaffolding — not 24/7 care |

## What a Strong First Place AZ Solution Looks Like

**Best fit problems to solve:**
- Employment retention — helping someone keep a job they have, not just get one
- Healthcare navigation — preparing for / communicating during appointments
- Daily transitions and routines — morning, work prep, meal management
- Social connection — reducing isolation outside of First Place's built community
- Self-advocacy — communicating needs to employers, doctors, or support staff

**Adult-appropriate design principles:**
- Interface must feel like an adult tool, not a children's app
- User must be in control (not a caregiver or staff member who controls it on their behalf)
- Privacy must be explicit — user decides what is tracked and stored
- Must work for people who DON'T live at First Place (scalability is how this wins)
- "Dignity of risk" means the tool can let someone try and adjust — not prevent failure

**Red flags:**
- Anything that requires a support worker to set it up or use it for the resident
- Solutions that feel clinical or medical (residents are tenants, not patients)
- Apps that assume the caregiver is the user
- Tools that only work inside a supported residential community

## SLIDE 1 — HOOK + PROBLEM (60 seconds)

**Opening line (memorize this):**
> "A teacher at an autism charter school in Scottsdale gives a six-step cooking instruction. Before she finishes the sentence, three students have already lost the thread. Not because they weren't paying attention. Because the human brain — specifically the working memory of an autistic or ADHD brain under task-load — doesn't hold verbal sequences the same way."

**Bridge:**
> "Every teacher we talked to at AZACS knows this. They've been building picture schedules by hand, on laminated cards, the night before class, for every routine they teach. There is no tool that handles an instruction that was never planned."

**Problem statement (crisp):**
> "The gap is real-time. Spoken instructions that were never prepared. That's what Step Cards solves."

---

## SLIDE 2 — PARTNER RESEARCH (45 seconds)

**AZACS:**
> "We visited the Arizona Autism Charter School. Their teachers run hands-on classes — cooking, robotics, drone. A teacher told us: 'I need to be able to give an instruction and have the visual support appear before the student has already moved on.' That's the exact spec we built to."

**First Place AZ:**
> "We visited First Place AZ, a supported-living and employment program for adults. Josh Munoz, their transition program director, told us: 'About half of our residents are employed. The ones who struggle most get a verbal instruction from a manager and they either freeze or they ask to repeat it — and asking to repeat it twice is the thing that gets someone fired.' Same problem. Different setting."

**Connector:**
> "Same neurological failure point — working memory under load — from age 8 to age 28. One engine. Two modes."

---

## SLIDE 3 — SOLUTION + DEMO (75 seconds)

**Setup:**
> "Step Cards listens to a spoken instruction. In under three seconds, it breaks it into numbered plain-language steps with pictograms matched to each action — customized to each student's reading level. The teacher reviews and approves before anything reaches a student screen."

**Live demo handoff (if Manan is demoing):**
> "Let me show you. [Manan demo: teacher speaks 'wash your hands before handling food ingredients', cards appear for three students at different reading levels, teacher taps approve, student taps through steps]"

**If no live demo — narrate the recording:**
> "On screen: a teacher speaks a six-word instruction. The app produces per-student step cards in under three seconds. Student one sees single-word pictogram cards. Student two sees four-word steps. Student three sees the full instruction. All from one spoken sentence."

**Adult mode:**
> "Same engine, different output. A worker pastes a manager's instruction. They get numbered steps, any ambiguous terms flagged in plain language, and a suggested clarifying question — so they can act without asking to repeat themselves."

**Fading:**
> "And here's the part that matters for independence: the app tracks how many times a student completes a routine. As they master it, the number of visible cards decreases. Support fades. That's built directly from NCAEP verified evidence — the same framework special educators use."

---

## SLIDE 4 — EVIDENCE + IMPACT (60 seconds)

**Evidence:**
> "Step Cards implements four of the 28 verified evidence-based practices from the National Clearinghouse on Autism Evidence and Practice 2020 review: Task Analysis, Visual Supports, Prompting with systematic fading, and Technology-Aided Instruction. This isn't a prototype built in a week. It's a product built on twenty years of special education research."

**Impact trajectory:**
> "Six-week pilot at AZACS. We measure steps completed independently before and after. We publish the outcome report. A replication guide so any school can run the same pilot. Then — Arizona Department of Education, CEC teacher networks, and First Place AZ Transition Academy for the adult track."

**The economic case:**
> "Every time a neurodivergent worker loses a job because of a preventable communication failure, the cost is absorbed by government vocational rehab programs, families, and the worker. Step Cards' long-term value isn't subscription revenue. It's reducing that cost."

---

## SLIDE 5 — BUSINESS MODEL + ASK (60 seconds)

**Revenue:**
> "Stage one: grant funding. IDEA funds assistive technology in special education. NSF SBIR supports edtech innovation. Stage two: per-teacher SaaS, benchmarked at two to five hundred dollars per teacher per year — comparable to Goally and Tiimo. Stage three: institutional licensing for employment programs and employer accessibility leads."

**Cost advantage:**
> "Our marginal cost per additional school is essentially zero beyond the LLM API call — about one tenth of a cent per instruction. Everything else — pictograms, speech recognition, hosting — is free."

**Closing ask:**
> "We have one partner ready to pilot. We have the evidence base. We have a working product. What we need is the runway to run the AZACS pilot, publish the outcome, and take that outcome to the next school."

**Hard close:**
> "Step Cards. Spoken instruction in. Independence out."

## DELIVERY NOTES

- Do not read from notes. These are prompts, not a script.
- The hook must be memorized — first 15 seconds decides the room.
- Pause after "That's what Step Cards solves." Let it land.
- If the demo fails: narrate from memory. Keep moving. Do not apologize.
- Q&A likely questions:
  - "Why can't a teacher just use existing visual schedule apps?" → Answer: every existing tool requires the teacher to build the routine ahead of time. Step Cards handles unplanned instructions in real time. That's the gap no existing tool fills.
  - "What about privacy?" → Answer: speech goes to the browser's built-in API, no student names or diagnoses ever leave the device in an API call. No backend. No student data stored off-device.
  - "Is this FDA-regulated as assistive technology?" → Answer: No. It's a productivity tool for teachers and workers, not a medical device. Same category as any edtech app.
  - "What does AZACS think?" → Answer: we were in their classroom. The teacher asked if she could keep using it after the hackathon.
